DESCRIPTION
In May 1949, the people of Shanghai, China's richest city, woke up to find Mao Zedong's soldiers asleep on the pavements. By most accounts, they had orders not to enter a single home.

Twenty-two years earlier, that army was fewer than a thousand beaten men hiding in the Jinggang Mountains. It survived the Nationalist purges, five encirclement campaigns, the Long March, the Japanese invasion and a civil war against an army four times its size, by fighting in a way Mao boiled down to sixteen Chinese characters: the enemy advances, we retreat; the enemy halts, we harass; the enemy tires, we attack; the enemy retreats, we pursue.

This is the story of China's guerrilla war: how it was won, what it cost, and why its rules spread from Vietnam to Peru to Nepal.

ACCURACY NOTES
- Numbers that historians dispute are given as ranges or with "by most accounts" / "by some estimates" in the narration itself.
- Long March: counts of those who set out run from about 80,000 to 86,000; fewer than 10,000 of Mao's column reached northern Shaanxi. Mao's 25,000 li (about 12,500 km) is the official distance; Ed Jocelyn and Andrew McEwen, who walked the route in 2003, measured about 6,000 km.
- Luding Bridge (May 1935): the official account of volunteers crawling across the chains under fire is told as party history; some historians and journalists doubt there was much of a battle.
- The Futian / AB League purge (1930–31): estimates vary widely; the narration says "thousands", the most conservative common figure.
- The siege of Changchun (1948): estimates of civilians who starved run from about 150,000 to over 300,000.
- Land reform (1947–52): estimates of those killed run from hundreds of thousands to millions.
- Nepal's civil war (1996–2006): estimates run from about 13,000 to 17,000 dead.
- Mao's "fish in the water" image is paraphrased from On Guerrilla Warfare (1937). "Political power grows out of the barrel of a gun" is from the party record of his speech on 7 August 1927.
- Che Guevara is deliberately not presented as a follower of Mao: he said he had not read Mao's book.
