# The Army That Ran Away and Won: China's Guerrilla War (1927–1949)

## Cold open
Shanghai, May, nineteen forty-nine. || Shanghai, May 1949.
China's richest city woke up to a strange sight:
Mao Zedong's soldiers, asleep on its pavements.
They had just taken the city.
By most accounts, they had orders not to enter a single home.
Twenty-two years earlier, this army was under a thousand beaten men,
hiding in the mountains.
Fifteen years earlier, it ran for its life,
and most of those who started that run never finished it.
Now it held China.
So how did an army that kept running away
win the most populous country on earth?
This is the story of China's guerrilla war.
{pause 2.4}

## The barrel of a gun
Shanghai, April, nineteen twenty-seven. || Shanghai, April 1927.
China's Nationalist leader, Chiang Kai-shek, turned on his communist allies.
His soldiers and gangsters hunted them through the streets.
By most accounts, hundreds were shot in the first days,
and thousands more across China in the months that followed.
The communists had tried to win China in the cities.
In the cities, they had just been destroyed.
That August, a farmer's son from Hunan spoke at a secret meeting.
His name was Mao Zedong.
Political power, he said, grows out of the barrel of a gun.
In September, he led a peasant uprising in Hunan.
It was crushed within days.
Mao gathered what was left, by most accounts under a thousand men,
and led them towards the Jinggang Mountains.
On the way, he gave them three rules.
One: obey orders.
Two: do not take a single sweet potato from the peasants.
Three: hand over everything taken from the rich.
Remember that sweet potato. It will matter later.

## The mountain
The Jinggang Mountains were bandit country.
Two bandit chiefs ruled them.
Mao recruited them instead of fighting them.
In April, nineteen twenty-eight, another survivor arrived. || In April 1928, another survivor arrived.
Zhu De, a former warlord officer, brought his own battered column.
Together, they formed the Fourth Red Army.
Zhu commanded the troops. Mao was its political chief.
They were outnumbered, outgunned, and short of everything.
So they learned to fight a different way.
By May, Mao later wrote, it fitted into sixteen Chinese characters.
Four lines of four.
The enemy advances, we retreat.
The enemy halts, we harass.
The enemy tires, we attack.
The enemy retreats, we pursue.
Watch these sixteen characters. The whole war is written in them.
The rules grew too.
The sweet potato became a needle and a thread:
do not take even those.
Pay for what you buy. Return what you borrow.

## Lure them in
By nineteen thirty, the Red Army had moved into southern Jiangxi. || By 1930, the Red Army had moved into southern Jiangxi.
Chiang sent an army to destroy it.
Mao did not stand and fight.
He pulled his men back, deep into villages that would hide them.
The enemy marched in after them, strung out and lost.
Then the Red Army struck, one column at a time.
Chiang tried three times, in nineteen thirty and thirty-one. || Chiang tried three times, in 1930 and 1931.
Three times, he failed.
The enemy advances, we retreat. The first line had worked.
In November, nineteen thirty-one, the communists declared a state of their own: || In November 1931, the communists declared a state of their own:
the Chinese Soviet Republic, with its capital at Ruijin.
It had its own laws, its own money, and its own army.
But inside it, something darker was happening.
Mao's men hunted for traitors in their own ranks.
Under torture, prisoners named others, who were tortured in turn.
By most estimates, thousands of soldiers and party members were shot.
The revolution was already eating its own.

## The blockhouses
For his fifth attempt, in nineteen thirty-three, Chiang changed the game. || For his fifth attempt, in 1933, Chiang changed the game.
Advised by German officers, he ringed the communists with stone blockhouses.
Thousands of them, joined by new roads.
Every month, the ring crept a few miles closer.
The ring tightened slowly, like a noose.
The Red Army now had new leaders. Mao had been pushed aside.
Moscow's man was a German communist called Otto Braun.
He ordered them to hold every hill and fight in the open.
They fought like a regular army,
and they lost like one.
Their losses were enormous.
By the autumn of nineteen thirty-four, the Soviet was dying. || By the autumn of 1934, the Soviet was dying.
The leaders decided to break out.
They did not yet know where they were going.

## The long retreat
In October, over eighty thousand people slipped through the ring at night. || In October, over 80,000 people slipped through the ring at night.
They hauled printing presses and heavy equipment on their backs.
By some accounts, Mao, sick with malaria, was carried on a stretcher.
At the Xiang River, the Nationalists caught them crossing.
By some estimates, the army shrank by half within weeks.
In January, nineteen thirty-five, the leaders met in the town of Zunyi. || In January 1935, the leaders met in the town of Zunyi.
Braun's tactics took the blame, and Mao was brought back into command.
Party history calls it the turning point. Historians are more cautious.
But the guerrilla rules came back.
The army stopped marching in a straight line.
It doubled back, feinted,
and crossed the Chishui River four times to shake off its hunters.

## The bridge
In May, the army reached the Dadu River, in the far west.
The crossing was the Luding Bridge: thirteen iron chains over a torrent.
Party history says the planks had been torn up,
and that a few dozen volunteers crawled across the chains under fire.
Some historians doubt there was much of a battle at all.
Either way, the army got across.
Then came the Snow Mountains, climbed in thin cotton uniforms.
Then the grasslands, swamps where men sank and never came up.
In October, nineteen thirty-five, Mao's column reached northern Shaanxi. || In October 1935, Mao's column reached northern Shaanxi.
By most accounts, fewer than ten thousand of them were left.
Mao called it twenty-five thousand li: about twelve thousand kilometres. || Mao called it 25,000 li: about 12,500 kilometres.
Two British walkers who retraced it measured about six thousand. || Two British walkers who retraced it measured about 6,000.
Either way, it was a very long way to run.
They had survived. But they were cornered, and starving.
And Chiang was coming to finish them.

## The kidnapping
In the summer of nineteen thirty-six, an American reporter reached their camp. || In the summer of 1936, an American reporter reached their camp.
His name was Edgar Snow.
His book, Red Star Over China, made Mao famous worldwide.
In December, he flew to Xi'an to order one last offensive.
But his generals there wanted to fight a different enemy: Japan.
Japan had seized Manchuria, the homeland of one of them, Zhang Xueliang.
On the twelfth of December, Zhang's troops stormed Chiang's quarters before dawn.
By most accounts, China's leader fled up a hillside in his nightclothes.
He was found hiding among the rocks.
The man who had hunted the communists for nine years
was now a prisoner.
Two weeks later, he was released.
The communist envoy, Zhou Enlai, helped broker the deal.
Chiang agreed, in effect, to stop the civil war and fight Japan.
Zhang Xueliang flew back with him, and spent decades under arrest.
The hunt was over. For now.

## Fish in the sea
In July, nineteen thirty-seven, Japan invaded China in full. || In July 1937, Japan invaded China in full.
Chiang's armies fought the great battles, and lost most of them.
Shanghai fell. Then Nanjing, where Japanese troops massacred civilians for weeks.
The communists' main force, renamed the Eighth Route Army, went somewhere else:
behind Japanese lines.
In September, at Pingxing Pass, it ambushed a Japanese column.
It was a small battle, but a famous one.
After that, the guerrillas mostly avoided big fights.
In a book that year, Mao compared the people to water,
and guerrillas to the fish that swim in it.
Japan held the cities and the railways.
The communists held the villages in between, and recruited.
The enemy halts, we harass. The second line had worked.

## Kill all, burn all
In August, nineteen forty, the communists tried something bigger. || In August 1940, the communists tried something bigger.
Over a hundred regiments struck Japanese railways, bridges and mines.
It was called the Hundred Regiments Offensive.
Japan's answer became known in China as the Three Alls:
kill all, burn all, loot all.
Villages suspected of helping guerrillas were wiped off the map.
By some estimates, the population of communist areas fell by almost half.
Mao drew a lesson: never show your full strength too early.
In January, nineteen forty-one, Nationalist troops ambushed the communist New Fourth Army. || In January 1941, Nationalist troops ambushed the communist New Fourth Army.
From then on, the alliance against Japan existed in name only.
In Yan'an, Mao purged his own party again, and rewrote its history.
When Japan surrendered, in August, nineteen forty-five, || When Japan surrendered, in August 1945,
the communists were ready.
In nineteen thirty-seven, the party had about forty thousand members. || In 1937, the party had about 40,000 members.
Now it had over a million.

## The cities fall
In nineteen forty-six, the civil war began again, in full. || In 1946, the civil war began again, in full.
On paper, it was no contest.
Chiang had over four million men, many with American weapons.
The communists had perhaps a million, counting militias.
In March, nineteen forty-seven, Chiang's army captured Mao's capital, Yan'an. || In March 1947, Chiang's army captured Mao's capital, Yan'an.
Mao simply walked away.
The enemy advances, we retreat. The oldest rule still worked.
Where they ruled, the communists seized land and handed it out.
Peasants' sons joined them in their hundreds of thousands.
Chiang's China was rotting: inflation, corruption, men conscripted at gunpoint.
In Manchuria, the communists besieged the city of Changchun for five months.
They let almost no one leave.
By most estimates, at least a hundred and fifty thousand civilians starved. || By most estimates, at least 150,000 civilians starved.
{pause 1.4}
In China, that siege is still rarely mentioned.
Then, from late nineteen forty-eight, came three giant campaigns. || Then, from late 1948, came three giant campaigns.
In the biggest, Huaihai, over half a million Nationalist troops were lost.
The enemy tires, we attack. The third line had worked.

## Not one needle
In April, nineteen forty-nine, a million communist troops crossed the Yangtze. || In April 1949, a million communist troops crossed the Yangtze.
Nanjing, Chiang's capital, fell within days.
The enemy retreats, we pursue. All sixteen characters, complete.
Then, in late May, Shanghai.
Remember the sweet potato?
Twenty-two years on, the rule faced its hardest test: China's richest city.
By many accounts, the troops slept on pavements, not in homes.
Shanghai had expected looting. It woke up to this.
It was the best advertisement the communists ever had.
On the first of October, nineteen forty-nine, at Tiananmen Gate in Beijing, || On 1 October 1949, at Tiananmen Gate in Beijing,
Mao proclaimed the People's Republic of China.
In December, Chiang fled to Taiwan.
The army that had run away for twenty-two years had won.

## The fish and the water
But the promise had limits.
In the land reform that followed, landlords were tried before crowds.
Estimates of the dead run from hundreds of thousands to millions.
Mao would rule China for twenty-seven years, at a terrible human cost.
Yet his guerrilla rules escaped China.
In Vietnam, they shaped the war against France, and then America.
In Peru, the Shining Path called itself Maoist.
In Nepal, from nineteen ninety-six, Maoists fought a ten-year "people's war". || In Nepal, from 1996, Maoists fought a ten-year "people's war".
By most counts, more than thirteen thousand people died. || By most counts, more than 13,000 people died.
In two thousand eight, Nepal's monarchy was abolished, || In 2008, Nepal's monarchy was abolished,
and the guerrilla leader Prachanda became prime minister.
Sixteen characters, from a mountain hideout,
are still studied in military academies around the world.
A guerrilla army needs the people, the way a fish needs water.
It wins by protecting them.
But once it has won,
who protects the people from it?
