# The Taiping Rebellion: the Failed Student Who Became God's Son (1851–1864)

## Cold open
In June, eighteen sixty-four, in the besieged city of Nanjing,
a man who believed he was the younger brother of Jesus
lay dying in his palace.
His city was starving.
By one of his own generals' accounts,
he told his people to eat wild plants, and call it manna.
His war against China's Qing dynasty had torn the country apart,
and, by most estimates, killed twenty to thirty million people.
This is the story of the Taiping Rebellion:
how a failed exam student built a kingdom of heaven,
and why it ended in fire.
{pause 2.4}

## The list
Canton, southern China, eighteen thirty-seven.
Thousands of young men crowded the city to sit the imperial exams.
Pass, and you could become an official: rich, respected, safe.
Fail, and you went home with nothing.
Hong Xiuquan was twenty-three, the son of Hakka farmers.
He had already failed twice.
That spring, the results were posted again.
His name was not on the list.
Remember that list. It will matter later.
Hong collapsed. He was carried home, and for weeks, he raved.
His family thought he was dying.
In his fever, he rose into a palace in the sky.
An old man with a golden beard handed him a sword,
and told him the world was overrun with demons.
A younger man, whom Hong called his elder brother,
helped him hunt them down.
For six years, the dream meant nothing at all.

## God's younger son
In eighteen forty-three, Hong failed for the fourth time.
Then he opened a book he had been handed years before:
Christian tracts by a Chinese convert named Liang Fa.
Reading them, Hong understood his dream. Or believed he did.
The old man with the golden beard was God the Father.
The elder brother was Jesus Christ.
Which made Hong the second son of God, sent to slay demons.
And the chief demons, he came to believe, were the Manchus,
the foreign dynasty that had ruled China for two centuries.
By most accounts, he threw the Confucian tablets out of his school,
and lost his teaching job.
In eighteen forty-seven, he studied in Canton with an American missionary,
the Baptist preacher Issachar Roberts.
But the two men fell out, and Hong left unbaptised.
He did not need a church. He already had a mission.

## Thistle Mountain
That mission found its home in the hills of Guangxi province.
One of Hong's first converts, Feng Yunshan, had gone ahead.
Near Thistle Mountain, he built a congregation: the God Worshipping Society.
Its people were poor, many of them Hakka, outsiders in their province.
And southern China was coming apart.
In eighteen forty-two, Britain had won the First Opium War.
Opium flowed in. Silver flowed out.
Bandits, pirates and hunger filled the gap the government left.
So the God Worshippers armed themselves,
and pooled their money in a common treasury.
By late eighteen fifty, thousands had gathered at the village of Jintian. || By late 1850, thousands had gathered at the village of Jintian.
The Qing sent soldiers to break them. The soldiers were beaten.

## The Heavenly Kingdom
On the eleventh of January, eighteen fifty-one, Hong's birthday,
the God Worshippers declared a new state:
the Taiping Tianguo, the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace.
Hong was its Heavenly King.
The men cut off the Manchu braid, and grew their hair.
The Qing called them the long-haired rebels.
Beneath Hong, five kings were crowned: East, West, South and North,
and one more, the Wing King.
The strongest was the East King, Yang Xiuqing, a former charcoal burner.
Soon the kingdom even had its own calendar.
Then they marched north.
In eighteen fifty-two, two of the five kings fell in battle.
But the army only grew, swelling with every town it took.
In January eighteen fifty-three, they took the Yangtze city of Wuchang. || In January 1853, they took the Yangtze city of Wuchang.
By some estimates, half a million people were now on the move.

## The Heavenly Capital
On the nineteenth of March, eighteen fifty-three, they took Nanjing.
By most accounts, the Manchu garrison and its families were massacred.
Hong renamed the city Tianjing: the Heavenly Capital.
He moved into a palace, and rarely left it again.
Outside, the new kingdom made laws no Chinese state had made before.
Opium was banned. So were foot-binding, prostitution and buying brides.
On paper, land would be shared equally, between men and women.
Women could serve as soldiers, and as officials.
The kingdom even held its own examinations, set on Hong's Bible,
by some accounts, with exams for women too.
The man who had failed the exams now wrote them.
But much of it never left the page.
Men and women lived apart, even husbands and wives.
And while followers were told to own nothing,
the kings kept palaces and harems.

## The scholar's army
The Qing's own armies were failing.
So the dynasty turned to a scholar from Hunan named Zeng Guofan.
Zeng was everything Hong was not:
a Confucian official who had passed every examination.
He raised a new army in his home province,
the Hunan Army, recruited village by village, loyal to its own officers.
After one early defeat, by most accounts, he tried to drown himself.
Meanwhile, the Taiping sent an army north, to take Beijing.
It came within striking distance,
then froze, starved, and was wiped out by eighteen fifty-five.
Along the Yangtze, the war settled into a grinding stalemate.
Cities changed hands again and again,
and each time, the people inside paid the price.

## The voice of God
Inside the Heavenly Capital, a different danger was growing.
Yang, the East King, had a gift.
He fell into trances, and spoke as God the Father.
And God, through Yang, could give orders even to the Heavenly King.
Once, speaking as God, Yang demanded that Hong himself be whipped.
In eighteen fifty-six, Yang wanted more.
Hong was hailed as Lord of Ten Thousand Years.
Yang was only Nine Thousand. Now he demanded the same.
Hong appeared to agree.
{pause 1.6}
Then, by most accounts, he secretly summoned the North King, Wei Changhui.
In the early hours of the second of September,
Wei's men stormed the East King's palace, and killed him.
Then they kept killing: Yang's family, his officials, his followers.
By some accounts, more than twenty thousand people died.
The Wing King, Shi Dakai, protested, and fled. His family was killed.
In November, Hong had Wei executed too.
And in eighteen fifty-seven, Shi left, with about a hundred thousand followers.
Of Hong's five kings, not one remained at his side.
The kingdom had done to itself what the Qing could not.

## A second chance
And yet, the kingdom did not fall. Not yet.
In eighteen fifty-nine, Hong's cousin, Hong Rengan, reached Nanjing. || In 1859, Hong's cousin, Hong Rengan, reached Nanjing.
He had spent years among missionaries in Hong Kong,
and welcomed foreign visitors, hoping the West would take his side.
Hong made him chief minister,
and he drew up a plan to modernise China:
railways, steamships, banks and newspapers.
And a gifted general, Li Xiucheng, the Loyal King, took the field.
In eighteen sixty, he smashed the Qing army camped outside Nanjing,
and swept east into the richest land in China. Suzhou fell.
Next stood Shanghai, and its foreign merchants.
The Taiping expected a welcome. They were Christians too, after all.
Instead, in August eighteen sixty,
British and French troops fired on them from the city walls.

## The West chooses
That same year, Britain and France were at war with the Qing.
In October, their troops looted and burned the Emperor's Summer Palace.
Then they made peace, on their own terms.
Now the foreign powers had a reason to keep the Qing alive:
a dynasty that paid its debts, and honoured its treaties.
Foreign officers began drilling Chinese troops with modern rifles.
An American adventurer, Frederick Townsend Ward, led the Ever Victorious Army,
until he was killed in eighteen sixty-two.
A British officer, Charles Gordon, took over.
But the real blow came up the Yangtze.
In September eighteen sixty-one, Zeng's Hunan Army took Anqing, || In September 1861, Zeng's Hunan Army took Anqing,
the fortress that guarded the river road to Nanjing.
By most accounts, after a long siege, its defenders were massacred.
In the spring of eighteen sixty-two,
Zeng's younger brother, Zeng Guoquan,
dug in outside the walls of the Heavenly Capital.

## Manna
The siege lasted two years.
Li Xiucheng begged Hong to abandon the city and break out.
Hong refused. God, he said, would protect it.
As the food ran out, according to Li's own confession,
Hong told his people to eat sweet dew: wild plants, heaven's manna.
In the spring of eighteen sixty-four, Hong fell ill.
On the first of June, the Heavenly King died.
His son said it was sickness. Li claimed poison.
Hong's teenage son became the new Heavenly King.
Seven weeks later, on the nineteenth of July,
Zeng Guoquan's men fired a mine dug beneath the walls.
About sixty yards of wall came down.
{pause 1.0}
The Hunan Army poured into the Heavenly Capital.
For three days, by most accounts, there was slaughter and fire.
Zeng Guofan reported a hundred thousand rebels dead.
That number may be exaggerated.
But the Heavenly Capital was no more.

## The price
Li gave the boy king his best horse, and was captured.
In his cell he wrote a long confession. Then he was executed.
The boy king was caught that autumn, and executed too.
Shi Dakai had been captured and put to death the year before.
Taiping armies fought on for a few more years.
But the Heavenly Kingdom was gone.
The cost was almost beyond counting.
By most estimates, twenty to thirty million people died,
from battle, massacre, famine and disease.
Perhaps more than in the whole First World War.
The Qing survived, but only by handing power to men like Zeng,
and to regional armies loyal to their generals, more than to Beijing.
And remember the list Hong's name never appeared on?
In nineteen oh five, the Qing abolished the imperial examinations altogether. || In 1905, the Qing abolished the imperial examinations altogether.
Six years later, the dynasty itself was overthrown.
The system that rejected Hong outlived him by just forty-one years.

## A kingdom carved in stone
In Tiananmen Square, in the heart of Beijing,
stands the Monument to the People's Heroes, finished in nineteen fifty-eight. || stands the Monument to the People's Heroes, finished in 1958.
Around its base run eight carved scenes from China's revolutions.
One of them is the uprising at Jintian, in eighteen fifty-one. || One of them is the uprising at Jintian, in 1851.
Sun Yat-sen, father of the nineteen eleven revolution, drew inspiration from them. || Sun Yat-sen, father of the 1911 revolution, drew inspiration from them.
Mao Zedong praised Hong as an early revolutionary.
Official Chinese histories still honour them as rebels against an empire.
And in Nanjing today, there is a museum of the Heavenly Kingdom.
But they followed a man who believed he was God's son,
into a war that killed more people than almost any other.
A revolution, or a catastrophe with a prophet at its head?
A century and a half later, China is still deciding.
